# Cat Digestive Health

## Not Eating (Anorexia)
Cats that stop eating for more than 24-48 hours are at risk of hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), which can be life-threatening. Even if the underlying cause is minor (stress, new food), prolonged food refusal requires veterinary attention. Never assume a cat is just being picky if the refusal lasts more than a day.

## Vomiting
Cats vomit more readily than dogs and occasional vomiting (hairballs, eating too fast) is common. A hairball once or twice a month is normal for long-haired cats. Vomiting more than once a week, vomiting blood, or projectile vomiting suggests a more serious problem such as intestinal obstruction, hyperthyroidism, or kidney disease.

## Diarrhea
Loose stools for one to two days after a diet change or stress are usually self-resolving. Diarrhea with blood, lasting more than 48 hours, or combined with vomiting and lethargy requires veterinary evaluation. Common causes include parasites, inflammatory bowel disease, and food intolerance.

## Hairballs
Frequent retching followed by producing a cylindrical wad of fur is a normal hairball. Retching without producing anything, especially if frequent, may indicate the hairball is stuck and could require veterinary intervention. Regular brushing reduces hairball frequency.

## Drinking More Water Than Usual
Increased water consumption in cats is a significant warning sign for kidney disease, diabetes, or hyperthyroidism — all common in middle-aged and older cats. Any noticeable change in water intake should prompt a vet visit and basic bloodwork.

## Straining in the Litter Box
Straining to urinate (not defecate) is an emergency in male cats — it can indicate a urethral blockage which is fatal within 24-48 hours if untreated. Signs include frequent trips to the litter box, producing little or no urine, crying, and restlessness. Seek emergency care immediately.
